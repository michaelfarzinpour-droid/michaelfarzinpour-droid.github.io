Mini-lesson 1: Turn a position into a persuasive speech

Illustrative asynchronous lesson · 12–15 minutes · No prerecorded video required · Adaptable to an approved course shell

Learning outcomes
  1. Write a specific, arguable thesis suitable for a defined audience.
  2. Organize two or three supporting points in a logical sequence.
  3. Use credible evidence and a spoken source citation.
  4. Create a transition and a concrete call to action.
Lesson sequence
  1. 2 min: Compare a topic with a persuasive claim.
  2. 3 min: Identify audience concerns and constraints.
  3. 4 min: Build a claim–reason–evidence outline.
  4. 2 min: Write transitions and conclusion.
  5. 2–4 min: Low-stakes check and revision.
Worked example: campus transit

Topic: Transit. Audience: students balancing jobs and coursework. Purpose: persuade the audience to support an evening shuttle pilot. Thesis: “Our campus should test a late-evening shuttle for one term because it could make evening classes easier to reach, and the pilot can be evaluated before expanding service.” This is a fictional practice claim, not an evidence-backed policy recommendation.

Outline: I. Identify the student transportation problem, using verified local evidence if available. II. Explain a limited pilot proposal and address cost concerns. III. Recommend a defined evaluation measure and action. Transition: “Having identified the access problem, we can consider a bounded way to test a solution.” Conclusion: “Ask the student government to explore a one-term pilot and publish its results.”

Source practice: Introduce an authentic source aloud by author or organization and date; do not invent statistics. Use approved citation guidance.

Micro-activity: revise a weak thesis

Weak thesis: “Public transportation is important.” Rewrite it for a specific audience, clear action, and bounded claim. Sample: “Our student association should survey evening students before recommending changes to campus transportation.” The sample is arguable, specific, and feasible without pretending a survey was completed.

Practice submission & feedback standard

Submit a 150–200-word speaking outline (not a replacement for any assigned speech) with audience, claim, two reasons, source plan, signpost, and action. A formative instructor comment should name one strength, identify one revision linked to the learning outcome, and offer one next step. Students can use a text outline as a planning aid before completing the required oral presentation.

Mini-lesson 2: Listen, check perceptions, and respond to conflict

Illustrative asynchronous lesson · 12–15 minutes · Text-first accessible alternative to spoken role-play

Learning outcomes
  1. Distinguish observation from interpretation.
  2. Use a paraphrase and an open question to demonstrate listening.
  3. Apply perception checking before assigning intent.
  4. Draft an assertive, respectful response to a disagreement.
Lesson sequence
  1. 2 min: Read a brief fictional conflict.
  2. 3 min: Separate observations from assumptions.
  3. 3 min: Compose a perception check.
  4. 3 min: Pair paraphrase with open-ended question.
  5. 2–4 min: Choose and justify a response.
Worked scenario: missed group meeting

Alex messages Jordan: “You never care about this project.” Jordan missed a meeting after a schedule change. The statement is an inference about motivation, not an observation.

Observation: “We did not connect at Tuesday’s meeting.” Two possible interpretations: “You may not have seen the calendar change, or the time may no longer work.” Clarification: “What happened from your perspective?” Active listening: “It sounds like the schedule changed while you were at work. Is that right?” Assertive next step: “I need advance notice when meeting times change. Could we confirm the next two times in one message?”

Teaching note: A useful perception check avoids mind-reading, does not demand personal disclosures, and gives the other speaker room to explain.

Micro-activity: identify an assumption

Statement: “Pat didn't reply for six hours, so Pat is ignoring me.” Observation: no response for six hours. Inference: deliberate ignoring. Rewrite: “I haven't heard back yet; is this a bad time, or should we pick another way to coordinate?”

Practice submission & feedback standard

Draft a three-part exchange: observed behavior, at least two plausible interpretations, and a clarification question; then write a paraphrase and a collaborative request. Assess the specificity of observation, openness of wording, and respect for boundaries—not whether the student discloses a personal experience. Fictional scenarios are acceptable.

Sample substantive discussion reply

“You argue that using technical terms may build credibility with specialists. How might those same terms affect a first-time audience? Choose one sentence from your draft and rewrite it for a newcomer. Which principle from the reading supports your revision?”

Sample student support message

“I see your question about the assignment's requirements. The published rubric identifies the dimensions being scored. Let's compare your outline to those criteria during office hours. If the instructions themselves conflict, I'll confirm the interpretation with the designated course contact and update everyone consistently.”

Faculty checklist

Before week: inspect approved content, links, captions, due dates, and rubric consistency. During week: announce priorities, engage substantively, check unanswered questions, and hold virtual office hours. After week: return actionable feedback, document problems appropriately, and raise course-shell issues through the prescribed academic channel.

Instructor's sample feedback to Sam

“Your opening makes a clear, appropriately limited proposal, and your conclusion gives listeners a concrete next step. To strengthen organization, add a signpost before your second point—for example, ‘Beyond access, we also need a way to evaluate the pilot.’ For the evidence criterion, identify the actual source, date, and relevant finding when referencing a study; if you cannot verify it, remove or replace that claim. For delivery, rehearse the middle section once with pauses marked in your outline. Your next revision priority is to verify the source and add the missing transition.”

Grading fairness & accessibility

Use the institution's actual approved criteria, weightings, and processes in a real course. Evaluate communication performance rather than presumed identity, accent, or disability. Route formal accommodation questions through designated channels. Record rubric-linked reasons consistently, offer clarification during virtual office hours, and use the published grade-review procedure for disputes.

Teaching philosophy: clear expectations, meaningful engagement, equitable support

My teaching starts with the learning outcomes. In an asynchronous communication course, students need an understandable path through approved readings, discussions, assignments, and assessment criteria. I would preserve the university's common course structure while bringing communication research and practical examples into announcements, discussion replies, and office-hour conversations.

I view substantive engagement as more than posting reminders. A useful instructor response names a student’s idea, connects it to a course concept, and poses a question that helps the student apply or reconsider the idea. Feedback should be equally specific: identify what is working, cite a visible rubric criterion, and recommend an achievable revision. For public speaking, that can mean helping students organize claims and practice delivery without bypassing the oral communication outcome. For interpersonal communication, it can mean modeling listening, perception checking, and respectful disagreement.

Student-centered teaching means recognizing different work schedules, prior academic experiences, and access needs while applying course policies consistently. I would offer predictable virtual office hours, accessible instructions and examples, private outreach when a student disengages, and referrals to approved support channels when appropriate. I would never promise unapproved grade changes or accommodations. I would promptly flag material errors, access barriers, and unresolved concerns to the designated academic or support contact.
