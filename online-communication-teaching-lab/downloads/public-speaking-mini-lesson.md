# Mini-lesson 1: Turn a position into a persuasive speech

Independent fictional teaching work sample — Michael Farzinpour, PhD. Not an Excelsior University course or institutional rubric.

Illustrative asynchronous lesson · 12–15 minutes · No prerecorded video required · Adaptable to an approved course shell

- Write a specific, arguable thesis suitable for a defined audience.
- Organize two or three supporting points in a logical sequence.
- Use credible evidence and a spoken source citation.
- Create a transition and a concrete call to action.

- 2 min: Compare a topic with a persuasive claim.
- 3 min: Identify audience concerns and constraints.
- 4 min: Build a claim–reason–evidence outline.
- 2 min: Write transitions and conclusion.
- 2–4 min: Low-stakes check and revision.

Topic: Transit. Audience: students balancing jobs and coursework. Purpose: persuade the audience to support an evening shuttle pilot. Thesis: “Our campus should test a late-evening shuttle for one term because it could make evening classes easier to reach, and the pilot can be evaluated before expanding service.” This is a fictional practice claim, not an evidence-backed policy recommendation.

Outline: I. Identify the student transportation problem, using verified local evidence if available. II. Explain a limited pilot proposal and address cost concerns. III. Recommend a defined evaluation measure and action. Transition: “Having identified the access problem, we can consider a bounded way to test a solution.” Conclusion: “Ask the student government to explore a one-term pilot and publish its results.”

Source practice: Introduce an authentic source aloud by author or organization and date; do not invent statistics. Use approved citation guidance.

Micro-activity: revise a weak thesis

Weak thesis: “Public transportation is important.” Rewrite it for a specific audience, clear action, and bounded claim. Sample: “Our student association should survey evening students before recommending changes to campus transportation.” The sample is arguable, specific, and feasible without pretending a survey was completed.

Which is the strongest persuasive thesis for a fictional campus audience?

Submit a 150–200-word speaking outline (not a replacement for any assigned speech) with audience, claim, two reasons, source plan, signpost, and action. A formative instructor comment should name one strength, identify one revision linked to the learning outcome, and offer one next step. Students can use a text outline as a planning aid before completing the required oral presentation.
