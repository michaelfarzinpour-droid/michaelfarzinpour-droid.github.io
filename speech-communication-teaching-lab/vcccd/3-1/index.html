Teaching philosophy and evidence

I approach communication instruction as a process of making a learning target visible, modeling the task, giving students supported practice, and offering feedback they can use to improve. In mixed-experience classrooms, I would vary participation pathways without lowering the stated outcome. Students can prepare a brief written response before sharing with peers, while all responses are evaluated against the same criterion: is the claim specific, supported, and responsive to the audience?

I want feedback to distinguish demonstrable performance from irrelevant assumptions about accents, background, personality, or a student's motivation. When students miss work, I would contact them privately, restate expectations, and direct them to the appropriate campus resources without asking for sensitive information. When an assessment pattern suggests a gap, I would revise instruction and check again, while avoiding causal conclusions about the effect of any one teaching change.

Professional foundation: I served as standalone online instructor for Communication Studies 311: Communication Technology Theory (Summer 2022) and Communication Studies 113: Small Group Communication (Spring 2023), alongside teaching assistant assignments in introductory communication, persuasion, communication theory, technology, and mass communication. The examples below extend that practice into hypothetical community college instructional settings.

Formal sample lesson plan

Illustrative course: Introductory Communication / Public Speaking · Lesson: Audience-centered persuasive claims · Length: 15 minutes

Prerequisite: Students can distinguish a broad topic from a claim, with optional review built into the model.

Materials: accessible text/slide handout with contrasting claims, audience cards, submission prompt, optional captioned explainer.

Learning outcomes:

  1. Draft a specific, actionable persuasive claim for an identified audience.
  2. Identify one piece of verifiable evidence needed to evaluate the claim.
  3. Anticipate one reasonable audience concern without stereotyping.

Assessment: Three-line exit ticket containing claim, source to verify, and audience concern. Each item is marked as demonstrated / needing additional practice using the published criteria.

Instructional approach: engagement and retrieval → instructor think-aloud → supported revision → formative feedback → exit ticket.

Accessibility: clear written instructions and readable sample text; private writing before voluntary speech; independent or paired practice; equivalent alternative participation where consistent with the approved syllabus and accommodation processes. Verify caption accuracy and keyboard accessibility when digital media are used.

Alignment rule: The official course outline of record, approved outcomes, grading and attendance requirements, and campus policies supersede this hypothetical plan.

15-minute sequence

0–2 min
Individual written response to fictional campus priority decision; optional verbal sharing.
2–5 min
Think-aloud contrasting a topic with an actionable audience-centered claim and missing evidence.
5–9 min
Independent or paired revision of a vague claim using an audience card.
9–12 min
Compare illustrative responses against three clear success criteria; give actionable revision feedback.
12–15 min
Exit ticket: one claim, one source to verify, one anticipated audience concern.

A 15-minute demonstration is a micro-lesson rather than a full class meeting. Actual lesson plans would be adapted to the scheduled course duration and curriculum.

Complete instructor demonstration script

0:00–2:00 · Connect

Instructor: “Imagine a fictional college can fund one improvement: evening tutoring, longer library hours, or an expanded study space. Take 30 seconds to write your recommendation and one reason. You can share it voluntarily, or keep your answer written for now.”

Possible student response: “More tutoring.”

Instructor follow-up: “That is a meaningful topic. What would the college actually do, and who specifically benefits?”

Alternative if no one volunteers: Display an invented response to analyze, rather than forcing unprepared public speaking.

2:00–5:00 · Model with a think-aloud

Instructor: “'Tutoring is important' names a topic. Try this: 'Our fictional college should pilot tutoring until 8 p.m. on two evenings each week because students with daytime commitments need another access option.' Now we have a proposed action, a decision-making audience, and a reason. What is still missing?”

Likely response: “Evidence that students need it.”

Instructor: “Exactly. We could seek de-identified tutoring usage by hour and a properly designed needs assessment. We should not claim that a survey has proved demand unless we can identify and verify it.”

Transition: “You'll practice the same move: from broad topic to a proposal that could be evaluated.”

5:00–9:00 · Guided practice

Instructor: “Your starting sentence is 'The library should be better.' Choose an audience: commuter students, fellow students, or the budget committee. Draft a concrete proposal, identify one source you would verify, and anticipate a concern. You may work alone or compare ideas with a partner.”

Possible response: “Extend library hours by two hours.”

Follow-up: “On which days? What would you check to establish demand and staff feasibility?”

Extension for faster learners: Ask how the claim would change for a peer audience versus a budget committee.

9:00–12:00 · Formative assessment and feedback

Instructor: “Let's use three visible criteria: specific action, evidence to verify, and ethical audience adaptation. Consider 'The library should stay open until 10 p.m. on weekdays.' The action is clear. To strengthen it, identify what data would demonstrate demand and what staffing concern needs addressing.”

Instructor to class: “What is one respectful change that would make the message more relevant to the budget committee?”

Possible response: “Suggest a limited pilot and compare its costs with usage.”

Instructor: “Good—that adds a feasible test without presenting the cost estimate as an established fact.”

12:00–15:00 · Exit ticket and closure

Instructor: “Please submit three lines: your revised claim, one source you would check, and one concern the intended audience could raise. I will use the responses to decide whether our next class needs more work on source credibility or claim specificity.”

Sample fictional response: “Pilot tutoring until 8 p.m. on Tuesday and Thursday. Verify demand with anonymized usage data and a student-needs survey. Address tutor staffing and cost.”

Closing: “A persuasive claim is a proposal we can examine critically, not just a strong opinion. Next we will practice verifying sources.”

Three-stage student support case

Fictional situation: A student has missed two discussion activities and reports competing work demands. No assumption is made about disability, motivation, or personal circumstances.

Stage 1: Private outreach

Within the course communication timeframe, identify the missing assignments neutrally, clarify where expectations appear in the syllabus, and offer office hours or a private message exchange.

“I noticed two assignments are not submitted. I'd like to help you identify the best next step while keeping course expectations clear. Please let me know whether an office-hours conversation or a message exchange would be easier.”

Stage 2: Agreed instructional plan

After a student conversation, identify a manageable next learning task and a syllabus-consistent due date. Example: complete an audience-analysis reflection by Friday, including a specific claim and evidence need. If exceptions or formal accommodations arise, follow the college's authorized process and refer appropriately.

“Let's focus first on the reflection's two learning criteria. Submit the claim and supporting-source idea through the approved course platform by Friday, consistent with the course policy. We will review the result against the same criteria used for the class.”

Stage 3: Follow-up and documentation

At the agreed check-in, evaluate the submitted work against course outcomes, acknowledge progress if observed, and decide whether further practice or referral is appropriate. Record only necessary academic facts in approved systems. Do not invent or promise learning gains.

Fictional branching result A: Work submitted and criteria met → provide targeted feedback and next assignment guidance. Result B: Work missing → ask about barriers without speculation, revisit next steps, and refer to appropriate campus support.

Sample accessible online learning activity

Topic: Identify and revise an assumption about a speech audience. Materials: captioned short video or equivalent text explanation, two fictional speech openings, a comparison worksheet, and a structured exit ticket. Provide accessible text and properly described links.

Prompt: “Identify one audience assumption in each example. Revise one sentence so it is relevant without treating all audience members as identical. Explain why your revision is more ethical or persuasive.”

Assessment: Check for identification, revision, and reasoning. The same three criteria apply to accessible response formats where permitted. Preview with keyboard-only navigation and check captions, heading structure, image text alternatives, and mobile readability before release.

Implement only with reference to the approved course outline, institutional accessibility requirements, and student-service procedures.

Hypothetical before-and-after teaching decision

Before: In a fictional class of 30, 24 submit an exit ticket; 15/24 identify an actionable claim (62.5% of submitters), but only 9/24 identify a credible source (37.5%). Six submit nothing.

Instructional change: Open the next class with a five-minute source-verification model, then guided practice distinguishing a traceable institutional report from an unattributed claim. Provide an independent response option consistent with course policy.

After (hypothetical illustration): 18 of 26 later submitted tickets (69.2%) identify a credible source. That is a descriptive increase of approximately 31.7 percentage points among submitters; the sets of submitters differ and there is no controlled design.

Responsible interpretation: The pattern could inform further teaching, but it is not evidence that this change caused improvement. Review the four missing later tickets separately and avoid assumptions about their causes.
