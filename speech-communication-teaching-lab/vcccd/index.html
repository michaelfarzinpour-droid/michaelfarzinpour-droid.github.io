Scenario 3 of 5 · Student support

Respond to missed assignments

A fictional student misses two discussion posts and reports difficulty balancing work with coursework. You have no verified details about the cause or any accommodations.

Decision: Choose an instructional response that supports learning and maintains standards.

Example outreach

“I noticed the last two assignments are missing. I'd like to help you identify a workable plan for completing the course objectives. Please review the syllabus and contact me during office hours or by message to discuss next steps. We can also identify relevant campus support resources. If you have an approved accommodation, we can coordinate its implementation through the appropriate process.”

Consult institutional policies before discussing exceptions, accommodations, attendance requirements, or referrals. Avoid requesting private medical information.

Scenario 4 of 5 · Online accessibility

Design an accessible online lesson

You are creating a Canvas lesson about audience analysis for students on varied devices and schedules.

Decision: Which design best balances learning objectives with access?

Practical accessibility checklist

  • Use structured headings and descriptive links; verify keyboard navigation.
  • Caption video and provide a text transcript or equivalent.
  • Make images understandable with appropriate alternative text.
  • Provide an accessible, equivalent participation pathway where course requirements permit.
  • Check materials with campus accessibility guidance and approved tools before release.

15-minute teaching demonstration

Audience-centered persuasive claims

Hypothetical introductory communication lesson. Students formulate an actionable claim for a specified audience, identify one piece of needed evidence, and anticipate an audience concern.

Success criteria: (1) Specific proposed action, (2) relevant reason or evidence need, (3) ethical audience adaptation without stereotyping.
0–2 min
Connect: Silent written response to a fictional campus decision: later tutoring, extended library hours, or more study space. Invite volunteers; do not require public disclosure.
2–5 min
Model: Compare “Tutoring matters” with “The college should pilot two evenings of tutoring access because students with daytime commitments need alternatives.” Distinguish assertion from verified evidence.
5–9 min
Practice: Students work individually or in pairs to revise a weak thesis for an assigned audience and flag a missing source.
9–12 min
Check: Invite spoken or written responses; apply the three visible success criteria and model respectful feedback.
12–15 min
Close: Exit ticket with revised thesis, source to verify, and audience concern. Use results to plan the following class.

Teaching choices and adaptations

  • Offer individual writing before oral participation and an accessible equivalent response route.
  • State criteria in advance and provide examples of stronger and weaker claims.
  • Do not represent the fictional campus needs or supposed surveys as actual institutional findings.
  • Adapt to the approved syllabus and course outcomes before classroom use.

Teaching philosophy

I approach communication instruction as a sequence of modeling, supported practice, feedback, and revision. I use transparent criteria and varied, accessible opportunities to demonstrate learning. My prior university teaching and work with student assessments inform this approach; the fictional cases here demonstrate how I would adapt it in a community college environment.

What the simulation does not establish

It does not verify learning gains, demonstrate experience at VCCCD, constitute an official course outline, or represent actual student performance. A hiring committee can use it to examine my instructional reasoning, but the examples remain hypothetical.
