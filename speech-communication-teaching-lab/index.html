Module 01 · Lesson design · 3 minutes

Build a persuasive speech lesson

Demonstrate backward design: define observable outcomes, select instruction, practice skills, then assess learning. This example introduces an audience-centered persuasive speech.

Learning outcomes
  • Write a specific, audience-centered persuasive thesis.
  • Organize a speech using a claim, supporting evidence, and reasoning.
  • Identify one way to adapt a message to an audience without stereotyping.

Choose the strongest opening activity

Scenario: A mixed-experience class includes confident speakers, first-time college students, and students anxious about speaking. You have a 50-minute session and want students to practice audience analysis.

50-minute sample class sequence

0–5 min
Low-stakes prompt: Who is the audience, and what decision might they face?
5–13 min
Compare two openings; identify claims and audience assumptions.
13–25 min
Mini-lesson on thesis, evidence, and ethical persuasion.
25–40 min
Pair practice: revise a thesis for a specified audience.
40–50 min
Exit ticket: submit a claim, supporting source idea, and audience adaptation.
Use a fictional or generalized issue, not a real student's personal information.
Instructor rationale: Paired analysis and an exit ticket create practice and observable evidence of learning before a higher-stakes presentation.
Interview-ready teaching demonstration

15-minute micro-lesson: audience-centered persuasive claims

This is a sample lesson for a hypothetical introductory Public Speaking class—not a claim of prior instructor-of-record experience teaching Public Speaking. Facilitate it live or walk a committee through the instructional reasoning.

0:00–2:00 · Connect

Ask: “Your college has funding for one improvement: later tutoring, more parking, or extended library hours. Which would you recommend, and what evidence would you need?” Invite silent writing before voluntary responses.

Instructor note: allow written participation and avoid forcing a cold-call response.
2:00–5:00 · Model

Contrast “Tutoring is important” (a topic) with “The college should pilot two late tutoring evenings because working students need access outside standard hours” (an actionable claim with a reason). Identify the proposed action, audience, and what must be verified.

5:00–9:00 · Guided practice

Give pairs or individuals an audience card: commuter students, college budget committee, or peer students. Have them revise a weak thesis, identify one needed piece of evidence, and name one possible concern.

9:00–12:00 · Share & assess

Invite two examples (spoken or written); assess against three visible criteria: specific action, relevant reason, ethical audience adaptation. Ask students what evidence is missing.

12:00–15:00 · Exit ticket

Each learner submits one revised thesis, one source they would seek, and one audience concern. Explain that responses guide the next class’s mini-lesson on research credibility and counterarguments.

“Our campus should expand evening tutoring. Some students work during the day, and missing tutoring makes school harder. A campus survey said students want more support, but I don't have the date with me. Expanding tutoring would help, and the college should do it.”

Full fictional assessment artifact

Model student persuasive speech: extended evening tutoring

Entire student speech, survey reference, and speaker observations are invented for instructional practice. No real campus data or outcomes are claimed.

Prompt: Deliver a 4–6-minute persuasive speech proposing an achievable campus improvement for a defined audience. The transcript is a fictional practice sample; evaluate oral delivery using the separate observer notes rather than inventing visual observations from text.

Introduction. [Speaker pauses briefly and looks at notes.] Imagine finishing a shift at 6 p.m., getting to campus at 6:30, and learning that the subject tutor you need left an hour ago. For students who work during the day, the time tutoring is offered can matter as much as the quality of that tutoring.

My claim is that our hypothetical community college should pilot two evenings of tutoring until 8 p.m. for one semester. I will explain why evening availability may matter, outline a limited pilot, and consider the cost and staffing concerns.

Point one: the access problem. Some students attend classes around work or caregiving responsibilities. If tutoring ends before those students arrive, available services are difficult to use. I saw an informal class poll suggesting that several classmates would welcome later hours. However, I did not record the poll’s sample size or question wording, so it should not be treated as reliable campus-wide evidence. Before advocating permanent changes, I would seek verified tutoring-center usage logs, an accessible student survey, and data on hours with unmet requests.

[Speaker reads two sentences directly from notes, then looks up.] This distinction matters because a compelling story can show a possibility, but it cannot estimate how common the need is. A real decision should use evidence from students who currently use tutoring and students who cannot access it. One way to do that would be to ask all enrolled students about their preferred hours, while recognizing that students who answer a voluntary survey may differ from those who do not. The college could also check whether students already seek help after the tutoring center closes, without assuming that every unmet request represents the same type of need.

Point two: a limited proposal. Rather than asking the college to expand every tutoring service at once, I propose a one-semester pilot on two evenings each week. The college could begin with a small set of high-demand subjects, communicate the schedule through accessible channels, and track attendance, appointment fill rates, waitlists, and student feedback. It could then compare patterns with existing hours, while noting that seasonal differences and self-selection limit causal conclusions. For example, a pilot evaluation could ask three questions. First, how many people used the evening sessions? Second, did those users report that they otherwise would have had trouble accessing help? Third, what subjects and formats were most useful? If usage is unexpectedly low, the college should check awareness and scheduling before declaring that the need does not exist. If demand is high, it should still examine cost per appointment and staff workload before expanding the pilot.

Point three: address counterarguments. A budget committee might reasonably ask whether enough students would attend to justify staff time. That is a real concern. A pilot should specify a staffing limit, minimum demand threshold, and a clear decision point before funds are committed for the long term. Online support might also help some students, but it would not solve every access problem, including internet availability or the need for in-person assistance. The college should ask students what mix of formats works. Another fair concern is that evening access alone does not help people who travel long distances or care for family members at night. For that reason, a pilot should not be described as a complete equity solution. It should be one option tested alongside remote appointments, appointment booking by phone, and accessible materials. College staff should review whether these options reach learners whose needs are not captured by existing attendance patterns.

Conclusion. Access is not only about whether tutoring exists; it is also about whether students can reach it. A one-semester, two-evening pilot would allow this hypothetical college to learn from documented demand before making a larger commitment. [Speaker ends quietly, without pausing after the final sentence.] Thank you.

Module 03 · Small group communication · 2 minutes

Facilitate a challenging group

Build on prior standalone instruction in Small Group Communication (University of Arizona, Spring 2023) to demonstrate how to manage participation and assess collaboration.

Group-work dilemma

Four students are preparing a short group presentation. Two dominate the discussion, one student contributes primarily in writing, and one has missed a meeting because of work. The group is frustrated.

Group-process check

RolesFacilitator, evidence checker, organizer, presenter; flexible, not fixed by identity.
AccountabilityShared product plus brief individual process reflection and contribution log.
Assessment principle: Separate the quality of the final presentation from evidence of collaboration. Avoid equating speaking frequency with contribution quality.
Multi-stage facilitation practice

Two-stage group conflict simulation

Stage 1: After a structured round, one member explains that unreliable internet prevented participation in a live planning call. Another argues that written contributions should count less than spoken ones.

Stage 2: accountability after the deadline

A student completes a draft late but provides documented work. Peers report uneven effort, although the final presentation is coherent. How should you assess?

Suggested learning evidence: group charter, decision log, individual reflection, and an aligned presentation rubric. Communication modes need not be identical to demonstrate contribution.

Teaching approach in practice

My teaching approach starts with explicit learning outcomes, low-stakes practice, constructive feedback, and transparent assessment. For speech courses, I want students to see communication as a learnable set of choices: define the audience, organize an ethical argument, support claims with credible evidence, listen carefully, and revise using feedback. I design options for participation and access while preserving essential learning goals. These examples represent my proposed teaching approach, not outcomes observed in a Kern classroom.

What this lab establishes—and what it does not

This lab offers concrete work samples in lesson planning, speech assessment, small-group facilitation, inclusive access, and online module design. It is not a claim of prior Public Speaking instructor-of-record service, teaching at Kern CCD, formal Canvas deployment of these exact modules, or observed student learning gains.
