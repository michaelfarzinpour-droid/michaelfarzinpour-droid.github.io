What this demonstrates

  • Outcomes-based planning: Match each scenario to an observable student task and rubric.
  • Equity-minded instruction: Offer consistent standards with accessible ways to prepare and participate.
  • Feedback cycle: Diagnose, give actionable feedback, and reassess with the same criteria.
  • Professional boundaries: Refer to approved campus processes rather than improvising disability or conduct determinations.

The instructional choices are practice prompts; different approaches may be reasonable depending on local policy and student context.

COMM 301 · Introduction to Public Speaking

Public speaking: from topic to delivery

Illustrative objectives: Construct a focused audience-centered thesis, organize a speech with appropriate evidence, and deliver an intelligible speech with effective support and ethical attribution.
COMM 311 · Argumentation and Debate

Debate: rigorous reasoning, respectful exchange

Illustrative objectives: Distinguish claims, warrants, evidence, and rebuttals; evaluate credibility and reasoning; and participate in structured argument under shared norms.
COMM 351 · Mass Media and Society

Mass media: framing and systems

Illustrative objectives: Compare framing across media texts, distinguish observation from interpretation, verify original sources, and explain how media structures can affect information production and circulation.

Instructor reflection

What I would carry into a real classroom

Start from the outcome, not the activity. Every lesson should produce observable work: a better thesis, a defensible warrant, or a supported media comparison. I would tell students what counts as evidence of learning, provide a model, and allow a targeted revision.

Maintain standards while expanding access. Clear written directions, captioned materials where appropriate, structured discussion, practice opportunities, and prompt referral to the institution's formal accommodation process can remove avoidable barriers without presuming what any student needs.

Make feedback actionable. A numerical score alone does not tell a student how to improve. I would pair criteria with one or two prioritized steps and a subsequent low-stakes opportunity to show growth.

Separate productive disagreement from personal attacks. In argumentation, I would assess the quality of claims, reasons, sources, and responses—not the student's ideology. I would set discussion norms, model strong counterarguments, and use applicable campus conduct policies when issues exceed routine facilitation.

Practice epistemic restraint in media literacy. My doctoral analysis of online communication reinforced the importance of distinguishing observable content from claims about intent or effects. In class, students would separate what a media text demonstrably says from what its producers or audiences might believe.
